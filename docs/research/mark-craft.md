# Logo craft: construction, optical correction, curves, lockups and small sizes

**Question.** What do practitioners actually do to make a logo well crafted? This covers construction grids and proportion systems, optical correction, curve quality, negative space, stroke and counter consistency, wordmark spacing and lockups, and behaviour at small sizes and in one colour. Which rules are evidenced, and which are folklore? The golden-ratio and Fibonacci-circle construction claims get particular scrutiny.

**Purpose.** Input to the spec for a Claude Agent Skill that designs brand identities. Two decisions bind this note. First, **hybrid drawing**: Python scripts supply geometry primitives, and the agent composes them. Second, a **render-then-rubric gate**: marks are rendered at several sizes and in mono, scored, and redrawn if weak. Every rule below is therefore stated as a check, either one a script can run (*script*), one the model judges by viewing a render (*model-visual*), or one only a person can judge (*human*).

## 1. Answer

**Conclusions first.**
- **Well-evidenced craft rules are about type-design optics and icon grids, not about logos as such.** The strongest sources are font-tool documentation (Glyphs, FontLab), type designers (Frere-Jones, Hoefler), one perception paper, and the Google, Apple and Microsoft icon guidelines. Logo practitioners (CGH, Pentagram) add only *tests* (black-and-white first, works at 16 px), not numeric rules.
- **Brand guidelines do state clear space in units of the mark** (Mastercard, Google, NASA). Published *minimum sizes* were hard to confirm, and **no primary source gives a mark-to-wordmark ratio**.
- **The golden ratio is folklore.** Twitter's own designer documents that its bird was built from circles, but not from golden-ratio circles. No primary Apple source documents phi. The Pepsi/Arnell document is a leaked pitch deck of uncertain authenticity. See §2.

## 2. Folklore verdict: golden ratio and Fibonacci circles

- **Apple.** No statement by Rob Janoff, or document from Apple, that the logo was built on phi was found. Janoff's own explanation, in his own words via Logo Design Love, is functional: "I took a bite out of it" so it would read as an apple, not "some other round fruit" ([Logo Design Love, Rob Janoff](https://www.logodesignlove.com/rob-janoff-apple-logo-designer)). Fast Company reports David Cole's debunking: the logo fits golden-ratio circles only in the way "any shape could be fitted to a series of circles" ([Fast Company, "Debunking the myth of Apple's golden ratio"](https://www.fastcompany.com/1672682/debunking-the-myth-of-apple-s-golden-ratio)) **(design press)**. Blogs report that Janoff denied using the golden ratio, but no primary interview containing that denial was found **(unverified)**.
- **Twitter (2012).** The primary source is Doug Bowman's launch post, which says the bird is "crafted purely from three sets of overlapping circles" ([Twitter blog, "Taking flight: #Twitterbird"](https://blog.x.com/2012/06/taking-flight-twitterbird.html); mirrored at [stopdesign.com](https://stopdesign.com/journal/2012/06/06/taking-flight-twitterbird.html)). The extract does **not** mention the golden ratio. Golden-ratio claims appear in vendor and blog posts ([logo.com](https://logo.com/blog/twitter-logo)). Design Shack measured the circles and found the geometry "almost but not strictly" golden ([Design Shack](https://designshack.net/articles/graphics/twitters-new-logo-the-geometry-and-evolution-of-our-favorite-bird/)). Accounts of how many circles were used conflict (13 or 15) **(unverified)**. Martin Grasser is credited with refining it ([Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/150097/twitters-original-blue-bird-logo-creator-martin-grasser-says-had-great-11-year-run)).
- **Pepsi (2008).** The "Breathtaking" document attributed to Arnell Group is dated 2008-08-04 and leaked in 2009. It frames the globe through the golden ratio, the Mona Lisa and Earth's magnetic field ([Creative Bloq](https://www.creativebloq.com/news/never-forget-that-utterly-ridiculous-pepsi-logo-design-document); [archive.org copy](https://ia902800.us.archive.org/15/items/pepsi-arnell-021109/pepsi-arnell-021109.pdf), not opened). Its authenticity has never been confirmed, and some think it is a hoax (same sources) **(unverified)**. Even if genuine, it is evidence of a *post-hoc rationale*, not of a construction method.

**Verdict.** *Constructing a mark from circles and arcs of a small, stated set of radii* is documented practice (Twitter), and it is consistent with icon keyline grids (Material). *Phi specifically* has no primary evidence in any of the three cases. Phi overlays are drawn after the fact, and with enough circles almost any curve can be fitted.

**What the spec should do.** Let the geometry library build marks from a declared radius set: integer ratios, a modular scale, or phi if the user asks. Record the actual construction so the presentation can show it truthfully. Never generate a "golden ratio" overlay for a mark that was not built that way. Bierut's warning that rationale is partly post-hoc ([Design Observer](https://designobserver.com/im-with-her/)) is the reason for this.

## 3. Construction and grids

- **Icon grids are the best-documented construction system.** Material system icons sit on a 24 dp grid with a 20 dp live area and 2 dp padding. Content may extend into the padding for visual weight but never past the trim ([Material system icons](https://material.io/design/iconography/system-icons.html)). Keyline shapes (circle, square, portrait and landscape rectangles) give equal *visual* size to different shapes ([m1 Style: Icons](https://m1.material.io/style/icons.html)). Product icons are edited at 192 dp so that edges land on 4 dp increments ([m2 Material iconography](https://m2.material.io/design/iconography)). Apple points to the grids in its production templates and tells designers to keep primary content centred to survive masking ([HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/app-icons)).
- **For logos, a grid is a construction aid, not a rule.** No logo practitioner source found prescribes a grid. The defensible transfer to logos is this: build in a declared unit, snap straight edges and centres to it, and allow optical deviations (rules 1–6) as *recorded offsets* from the grid.

## 4. Optical corrections

- **Overshoot.** Frere-Jones's *Typeface Mechanics* treats overshoot as graded, with the difference "driven by the difference in the curvature" ([001 reprint](https://stockholmstypografiskagille.se/typonyheter/typeface-mechanics-001/)). Glyphs puts overshoots in alignment zones: positive above flat tops and negative below the baseline ([Glyphs, PostScript autohinting](https://glyphsapp.com/learn/hinting-postscript-autohinting)). Its handbook says zones should be as small as possible and states a 25-unit maximum, with a default size of 16 if none is set ([Glyphs handbook, Font-wide hints](https://handbook.glyphsapp.com/postscript-hinting/font-wide-hints/)). At a 1000-unit em that is ≤ 2.5 %, consistent with the 1–3 % figure ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overshoot_(typography)), **(unverified)**). Practitioner forum opinion says display sizes need less overshoot than text ([Typedrawers](https://typedrawers.com/discussion/comment/13064)) **(unverified)**. That matters because logos are often shown large.
- **Stroke compensation.** The only peer-reviewed source found here is de Waard, Van der Burg & Olivers (2019). They find a horizontal line is perceived as thicker than an equal vertical one, by ~5 % and ~3 % in their two experiments ([PMC6802759](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6802759/)). Hoefler gives "nearly 7 %" as the vertical excess that makes a square look even ([H&Co newsletter](https://emails.typography.com/2019_01/index.html)). Forum posts quote measured differences of 13 % in Futura and 20 % in Avenir, larger than the illusion explains ([Typedrawers](https://typedrawers.com/discussion/comment/38749)) **(unverified)**. **So compensation is real, but its size is a style choice above a perceptual floor of ~3–7 %.**
- **Joins and ink traps.** Frere-Jones notes that weight piles up where strokes cross unless they are tapered ([002 reprint](https://stockholmstypografiskagille.se/typonyheter/typeface-mechanics-002/)). Bell Centennial's ink traps were cut to fill with ink on newsprint at small sizes ([Wikipedia, Bell Centennial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_Centennial)) **(unverified, secondary)**. On screens there is no ink spread, but anti-aliasing produces the same darkening at joins in small renders. That is an inference, testable by rendering.
- **Circle vs square, and optical centring.** These are covered by the Material keyline ratios (rule 5) and Material's permission for geometry-preserving "optical corrections" (rule 6). No documented offset value for triangles was found.

## 5. Curve quality

- **Extrema, economy and inflections.** Glyphs recommends nodes at horizontal and vertical extremes, using Paths > Add Extremes. Use "as few as necessary but enough to hit every extreme", because too many points make outlines bumpy at large sizes. Skipping nodes at inflection points is often better ([Glyphs, Drawing good paths](https://glyphsapp.com/learn/drawing-good-paths/)). FontLab adds that shallow curves need no extrema, and that handles belong inside the "magic triangle" ([FontLab](https://help.fontlab.com/fontlab/8/tutorials/calfonts/1.%20Drawing/01z%20Masterclass%20Better%20Vector%20Drawing%20Part%202)).
- **Continuity.** G1 means a shared tangent, and G2 a shared curvature. Speed Punk draws curvature combs, and a jump in the comb at a node marks a curvature discontinuity ([yanone.de](https://yanone.de/software/speedpunk); [GitHub](https://github.com/yanone/speedpunk)). FontLab says fonts usually aim for G2 ([FontLab](https://help.fontlab.com/fontlab/8/tutorials/calfonts/2.%20Honing/2c-2%20Project-%20Smoothing%20Out%20Pencil%20H)). **A tension for this skill:** marks built from circle arcs and lines (the Twitter method) are G1 but *not* G2 where a line meets an arc. Type designers smooth these joins; geometric logos often keep them. Rule 10 must therefore be switchable per concept.

## 6. Negative space and figure-ground

- The canonical example, the FedEx arrow between "E" and "x", is reported to have come from spacing adjustments, with Lindon Leader saying the arrow was not his starting goal ([Retail Brew](https://retailbrew.com/stories/2022/10/07/logo-big-or-go-home-how-fedex-s-secret-arrow-was-created-accidentally); [TheSneeze interview](https://thesneeze.com/the-man-behind-the-fedex-logo/), not reached) **(unverified)**. CGH's Princeton University Press mark puts a second "P" in the negative space of the first ([CGH](https://cghnyc.com/work/project/princeton-u-press)).
- **What can be checked:** counters and gaps must survive at small size (rule 14) and in mono (rule 17). Whether the figure-ground reading lands, and whether it reads unintentionally (an accidental shape), is **model-visual first, human last**. No numeric rule was found.

## 7. Consistency of strokes and counters

Material treats a consistent stroke as the default and documents deviations, for example 1.5 dp where 2 dp would clog ([Material system icons](https://material.io/design/iconography/system-icons.html)). Glyphs notes that glyphs with similar shapes share similar sidebearings ([Glyphs handbook, Spacing](https://handbook.glyphsapp.com/spacing/)). Both argue for *few, deliberate values*. The cluster limits in rule 13 are proposed defaults, not documented ones.

## 8. Wordmark and lockup

- **Spacing.** Glyphs starts with n and o (or H and O). The n gets about 1.5× the o's sidebearings, and kerning comes only after spacing can improve no further ([Glyphs, Spacing](https://glyphsapp.com/learn/spacing)). A wordmark is a fixed string, so spacing and kerning collapse into pair-by-pair optical spacing. Rule 20's area-evenness metric is a proxy for that, not a documented standard.
- **Clear space.** Brand guidelines express it in a unit of the mark: Mastercard ¼ circle width; Google the height of the "G"; NASA "2 N's", which conflicts with a "1N height" on another NASA page (all in rule 18). Google's archived 2008 rule used a fixed 25 px instead ([UNT web archive](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/eot2008/20081204202055mp_/http:/www.google.com/permissions/guidelines.html)). The move from a fixed pixel value to a mark unit is itself a finding.
- **Minimum size.** Current Mastercard pages state the minimum only in images. The 2016 PDF figures could not be tied to a specific lockup (rule 19). No official Airbnb Bélo minimum was found. An Airbnb merchandise guide gives a *maximum*: the Bélo should "never exceed 1 inch" on apparel ([Airbnb Goods Toolkit PDF](https://images.brilliantmade.com/uploads/attachment/file/154157/Merch%202025.pdf)) **(unverified as an official Airbnb document)**.
- **Mark-to-wordmark ratio: no primary source.** Pentagram's 2016 Mastercard work moved the wordmark outside the circles and later dropped it ([Pentagram, Mastercard](https://www.pentagram.com/work/mastercard/story); [Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/3061799/mastercard-gets-its-first-new-logo-in-20-years)), but no ratio was published.

## 9. Small sizes and one colour

- **Tests practitioners report.** Haviv checks marks at 16×16 px ([Logo Design Love](https://www.logodesignlove.com/sagi-haviv-interview)). Geismar starts in black and white because colour can hide weak form, and shows marks very small and very large ([LogoGeek, Tom Geismar](https://logogeek.uk/podcast/tom-geismar)).
- **Platform rules.** Apple: avoid "extremely thin line weights and sharp corners", and use "a minimal number of shapes"; text in icons is "often too small to read easily" ([HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/app-icons)). Windows: ship at least 16, 24, 32, 48 and 256 px ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/apps/design/iconography/app-icon-construction)). Visual Studio: 16 px icons have at most two elements ([Microsoft Learn, VS images and icons](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/visualstudio/extensibility/ux-guidelines/images-and-icons-for-visual-studio)), which is product-specific.
- **Pixel-grid alignment.** Material's 4 dp edge increments and Fluent's alignment to a four-pixel grid ([Fluent 2 layout](https://fluent2.microsoft.design/layout)) are the documented forms. Fluent's per-size pixel-snapping rules were not confirmed ([third-party listing](https://axialis.com/icongenerator/stock-icons/fluent-system)) **(unverified)**. Google says it made its logo "pixel perfect everywhere" with hand-tuned variants ([design.google](https://design.google/library/evolving-google-identity/)).

## 10. Implications for the skill (inference: my reasoning, not sourced)

- **Script checks (deterministic, run on the SVG or a high-resolution raster):**
  - overshoot extents (rules 1–2);
  - horizontal/vertical stroke ratio and join thickness (3–4);
  - circle/square size ratio and centroid offset (5–6);
  - missing extrema, redundant nodes, G1 angle, G2 curvature jump and handle triangle (7–11);
  - grid snapping and padding (12);
  - stroke and radius clustering (13);
  - minimum stroke and counter width at 16, 32 and 48 px renders, plus SSIM against the large render (14–15);
  - region separability in mono and reversed (17);
  - clear-space and minimum-size emission (18–19);
  - pair white-area evenness in wordmarks (20).
- **Model-visual rubric (the model views renders):**
  - Does it look equally tall and centred?
  - Do the joins clog at 16 px?
  - Is it recognisable small?
  - Does it still read in mono and reversed?
  - Does the figure-ground read as intended, with no unintended shape?
  - Is the wordmark spacing even when blurred?
  - Does the lockup feel balanced?
- **Human only:** whether a small-size variant may differ (16); the lockup ratio (21) if the defaults are rejected; whether a metaphor in negative space lands; trademark similarity.
- **Primitives the geometry library would likely need (suggestions only; the interface is a later decision):**
  - `overshoot(shape, ref_lines, pct)`;
  - per-axis stroke offsets (`stroke(path, w_vertical, w_horizontal)`) for compensation;
  - join tapering and notching (`taper_join`, `ink_trap(depth, angle)`);

## 11. Open disagreements and gaps

**Disagreements**
1. **Simplify small, or stay faithful?** Google and Material add weight or drop detail at small sizes. Older Microsoft guidance says small icons should be "as similar as possible" to large ones (rule 16).
2. **G2 everywhere vs geometric G1.** Type tools aim for G2. Circle-built marks (Twitter) are inherently G1 at line–arc joins.
3. **How much stroke compensation?** The perceptual effect is ~3–7 %, yet geometric typefaces reportedly use 13–20 % **(unverified)**. This is a style choice, not one number.
4. **Overshoot at display sizes.** Practitioners say less at large sizes **(unverified, forum)**, but logos are shown at every size, so one master must compromise.
5. **NASA clear space.** "2 N's" and "1N height" conflict across two NASA pages.

**Gaps**
- **No primary source** for a mark-to-wordmark ratio, a lockup gap, a canonical favicon pass criterion, or a numeric optical-centring offset.
- **Minimum sizes:** no current, unambiguous published figures were confirmed for Mastercard, Google or Airbnb.
- **Not reached:** the full Frere-Jones posts (seen only via a Swedish reprint), the Glyphs tutorials, Material pages (extracts only), FontForge's documentation on extrema and curvature (not found in extracts), Fluent iconography detail, the NASA 1975 manual's worm specifications, TheSneeze FedEx interview, a primary Janoff statement on the golden ratio, and the Arnell PDF itself.
- **Perception research:** only one primary paper was found (stroke thickness). There is no primary perception study for overshoot or circle/square size here; the evidence for those is practitioner documentation and keylines.
- **All thresholds marked "proposed default"** need calibration. Run the checks on a set of known-good marks before trusting them as gates.
